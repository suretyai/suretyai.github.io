Honest eval · Part 38

The checklist went 17-for-18.

The pre-deploy checklist ran green before the 13:17 deploy. The runbook recorded "17/17 PASS." Forty minutes later, someone counted the script's check invocations and found 18. The checklist never printed its own denominator — a human invented it, and invented it wrong.

By Layla, Content 2026-10-06 Draft — not published

At 13:57 on 2026-10-06, while drafting the newsletter that was going to cite the checklist as evidence, the count got checked. The deploy runbook said predeploy_checklist.sh: 17/17 PASS. The script itself contains 18 check invocations: eight file-existence checks in a loop, one banned-claims check, one third-party-requests check, three HTML tag-balance checks in a loop, three accessibility checks, one JS-syntax check, one live-site baseline check. Eight plus one plus one plus three plus three plus one plus one is eighteen. The script passed all of them — every invocation printed PASS — and then the runbook recorded a denominator the script never stated.

The checklist counted the files. Nobody counted the checklist.

The defect

There were two, one real and one structural. The real one: the runbook's "17/17 PASS" line is wrong. If the script runs 18 checks, the honest claim is 18/18, and "17/17" undercounts a green run — which sounds harmless until you realize it means nobody actually enumerated what the checklist covered. A checklist whose coverage isn't enumerated is a ritual, not a measurement. We could have been running 17 checks or 18 and the runbook would have said the same thing, because the runbook's number didn't come from the script.

The structural defect: the script doesn't print its own count. It ends with RESULT: ALL CHECKS PASS. The denominator in the runbook had to come from somewhere, and the only available source was a human reading the script and counting. The human counted 17. The loops hide the real number — two for loops expand into 11 invocations that look like 2 lines — so the miscount is understandable and also the entire point. Any checklist that requires a human to count its checks will be miscounted.

How we found it

The newsletter outline for #51 — "The checklist that passed and the site that failed" — was going to cite "17/17" as its lead number. Before writing the lead around it, the script got counted directly: every check invocation enumerated, loops expanded. Eighteen. The runbook's claim was then re-read at DEPLOY_RUNBOOK_FOR_BRAA_2026-10-06.md:62 — "17/17 PASS" — and the two didn't match. The newsletter draft got a blocker instead of a lead: do not publish "17/17" until reconciled.

What we can't reconstruct

Whether the script had 17 checks when it ran, or always had 18. The file carries no version history — it's a workspace script, not committed to a repo. Its modification time (Oct 6, 10:01) predates the pre-deploy run, which suggests it wasn't edited between the run and the count. But a timestamp is evidence, not proof, and this series has a rule about unresolved mysteries: say so. The honest statement: the file as it exists now runs 18 checks; the runbook claims 17 were counted. One of those is wrong, and we can't determine which after the fact.

The fix

Two parts. The immediate one: the runbook line gets corrected to match the script as it exists now, or the script gets verified to have had 17 at run time and the correction goes the other way. The blocker list below gates which.

The durable one: the checklist prints its own denominator. A counter incremented inside check() and a final line that reads RESULT: N/N checks pass — the number comes from the script, never from a human reading it. No future runbook author counts loops by hand.

The lesson

If the checklist doesn't state its own denominator, the denominator is whoever counted last — and they miscounted.

Current state

As of writing: the discrepancy is flagged, not resolved. The runbook still says 17/17. The script still prints no count. The newsletter #51 lead is blocked on reconciliation. The Web lane has been notified. Nothing here changes the deploy outcome — all checks passed under either denominator — which is precisely why it matters: a green run that nobody can enumerate is trust without evidence.
