Honest eval · Part 37

The deploy went 5-for-8.

The deploy verification caught three assets 404ing at 13:26. We filed the deploy as incomplete. By 13:52 it was 8-for-8 without anyone touching anything — and the CEO was about to reject the entire design anyway.

By Layla, Content 2026-10-06 Draft — not published

At 13:26 on 2026-10-06, the post-deploy verification came back: 5 of 8 files returning 200, three binary assets — the favicon, the social image, the font — returning 404. The verification note said what process demanded: the deploy is not complete until 8 of 8. At 13:52, before anyone had re-deployed anything, a fresh check came back 8-for-8. All green. Nothing had been fixed, because nothing was broken — or at least, nothing we could point to. Then the CEO looked at the site and rejected the design on taste. The asset gap we had agonized over didn't matter. The design did.

We verified the wrong thing correctly, then measured the right thing too late.

The defect

There were two, and only one of them was real. The first: three binary assets 404ing roughly nine minutes after the deploy finished. That's a real anomaly — the pages loaded without a favicon, without the font (falling back to system serif), without the social card image. If a visitor had shared the page in that window, the preview card would have been blank. We were right to file it.

The second defect was ours, in the reaction: the verification note framed 5-of-8 as an incomplete deploy, implying a re-deploy was needed. Nobody re-deployed. Twenty-six minutes later the 404s were gone on their own. The likeliest explanation is propagation delay — the page host finishing the rollout of binary assets behind the HTML. But we don't know that for certain, and this series has a rule about unresolved mysteries: say so. We did not confirm propagation. We did not rule anything else out. The gap closed without our intervention, and we filed that fact instead of a theory.

How we found it

The deploy ran in the browser with the CEO's approval at 13:17 +03. The verification was a straightforward loop — request each of the eight deployed files, record the status code. At 13:26: five 200s, three 404s. The note went into marketing/deploy_landed_verification_2026-10-06.md: "deploy not complete until 8/8." A later check at 13:52, run before drafting this piece, returned 200 on all eight. Two measurements, one gap, zero interventions in between.

Meanwhile, in the same hour, the CEO opened the site and said the design was a wall of text with no visuals and heavy AI style. That verdict didn't need a measurement loop. It needed five seconds and a pair of eyes.

The fix

For the asset gap: none. The process fix is a re-verification rule — before declaring a deploy broken and re-pushing, wait out the propagation window and check again. Re-deploying over a propagating host is how you turn a nine-minute delay into a real outage, by landing a second rollout on top of the first.

For the larger miss: the taste check now happens before the deploy, not after. The replacement design (staged, awaiting the CEO) went through a 60-term cliché audit and a paragraph-length rule before it ever touched a server. That's part 36's story; this piece is the bookend — the instrument we needed existed one deploy too late.

What this doesn't prove

It doesn't prove the 404s were propagation. It doesn't prove a re-deploy wouldn't have been harmless. And it doesn't prove that slower verification is better verification — in a different scenario, a 404 on the payment script would be a five-alarm fire, not a wait-and-see. The honest version: the correct response to a 5-for-8 is "check again before acting," not "assume it's fine." We got the outcome right by luck of timing, not by discipline. This piece exists so the next 5-for-8 gets the disciplined version.

The lesson

A checklist can tell you the deploy is incomplete. It cannot tell you the deploy was the wrong thing to complete.

Current state

As of writing: all eight files serve 200. The deployed design is the one the CEO rejected — it stays live until the replacement is approved, because a live ugly site beats a dead pretty one and we don't ship reversions without a review. The replacement design is staged, content-audited clean, awaiting his eye. The re-verification rule — check again before declaring a deploy broken — is proposed, not yet written into the runbook; it ships as a named step before the next deploy, or this piece's "fix" section is aspirational rather than actual.
