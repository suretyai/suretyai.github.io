Honest eval · Part 21

The machine can escalate. Judgment needs its own instrument.

Our dry-run daemon escalates everything it isn't sure about. The human verdict count stands at exactly zero — and the human side of the system is still a blueprint.

Fronesa engineering October 6, 2026 5 min read

The other side of the screen

Every post in this series has been about the machine: its recall, its calibration, its false positives, its dry run. This one is about the other side of the screen — the reviewer.

Our dry-run daemon has now spent ~8.5 hours classifying Bluesky firehose traffic and escalating everything it isn't sure about. Every decision so far is review-needed. The pipeline is alive, the escalation mapping holds. And the human verdict count stands at exactly zero.

That zero is honest. The human side of the system is still a blueprint, and we are saying so instead of implying otherwise.

What exists

A reviewer-trust instrument, built in four parts: a survey and task protocol, a feasibility check against our ledger, a survey-store design, and an onboarding protocol for the pilot that runs it.

The instrument measures trust in a decision-support system along three dimensions: perceived reliability, escalation judgment, and dependence stance. It is not a vibe check. It is a survey plus a task protocol, with the analysis specified before any data is collected.

The onboarding protocol includes the exact words a reviewer hears when they sit down — and the words they never hear. The framing is decision support, always. Not moderation. Not an algorithm to agree with. The script says: your job is to catch the system's mistakes, not to confirm its verdicts. Sometimes you will see what the system decided; sometimes you will not. Your scores measure how the system is doing at supporting you, not how you are doing. You can stop at any time.

There is even a stop rule for the framing itself: if any reviewer reports the items feel "like a test they can fail," the sessions halt and consent is repaired before anything resumes.

The measurement comes before the result, and the measurement has to be built right, or the result means nothing.

Why it has to exist before the first session

The ledger needs four new fields before a single reviewer sits down: display_condition, gold_label, review_started_at, session_id. The protocol is explicit about why: instrument data collected on the old schema cannot be retro-fitted. A session run without these fields permanently loses the automation-bias and latency analyses.

This is the same honesty discipline as the rest of the series: the measurement comes before the result, and the measurement has to be built right, or the result means nothing.

What does not exist yet

No sessions. No survey responses. No trust scores. The legal companion drafts (participant privacy notice, DSAR coverage) are written by non-lawyers and carry counsel-must-review banners. Nothing runs until the ledger fields land, the survey store exists, and counsel clears the consent text.

The machine side of the dry run is producing data every second. The human side is producing drafts. Both are reported here as exactly what they are.

The lesson

Build the instrument before the first session — data collected on the wrong schema cannot be retro-fitted, and a blueprint reported as a blueprint is honest.

Pre-publish blockers

  1. The reviewer-trust instrument trilogy + onboarding protocol remain DRAFT — do not describe them as deployed, piloted, or validated.
  2. Do not imply any reviewer has seen Fronesa output or given feedback. The honest number today is 0 human verdicts.
  3. The TCQ-12 dimensions (perceived reliability / escalation judgment / dependence stance) must be named exactly as in the instrument design; do not rephrase into marketing language.
  4. Counsel must clear the consent/withdrawal material before the protocol is described as "ready."
  5. The four ledger-extension fields are a precondition, not a footnote — keep them in the piece; dropping them makes the instrument sound easier than it is.
