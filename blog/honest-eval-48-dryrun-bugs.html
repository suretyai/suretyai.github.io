Honest eval · Part 31

We found three bugs in our own dry run before launch.

Our Bluesky labeler hasn't emitted a single public label yet. Good — because this week our own dry run caught three defects that would have made our launch metrics meaningless.

Fronesa engineering October 6, 2026 6 min read

Our Bluesky labeler hasn't emitted a single public label yet. Good — because this week our own dry run caught three defects that would have made our launch metrics meaningless. We're publishing the misses, not just the fixes.

Bug 1: We were only logging 8% of our traffic

The dry-run logger had a guard: if (!labels.length) continue; — skip any post that received no labels. Sounds like a tidy optimization. It meant our JSONL log only contained posts the model felt strongly enough about to label.

Run stats told the real story: 1,728 classified, 138 proposed. Roughly 8% of traffic was visible to every metric we computed from the log. The other 92% — the quiet majority, mostly ham — was invisible. Any precision, recall, or volume number derived from that log alone was computed on a filtered sample and was invalid.

The fix (one restructure of _flush() in labeler/src/pipeline.js, commit 4008d87): log every classified post before the empty-labels guard, keyed on an isLivePublish flag so live-mode publish semantics are unchanged. Verified live after the daemon restart — empty-label rows now appear in the log.

Bug 2: Our spam label could never fire

SPAM_CONFIDENCE was set to 0.90. The model's observed confidence ceiling in the dry run was about 0.80; the highest spam-decision confidence logged was 0.792. Zero spam labels were emitted across the entire run — not because there was no spam, but because the threshold sat above anything the model ever outputs.

Worse, escalated spam decisions were mapped to review-needed by design, so even confident spam calls took the other path. In live mode, the labeler would have emitted exactly one label type forever. The alert/blur/warn spam label — the core product — was dead code in practice.

The threshold recalibration is in progress against the real confidence distribution (spam p50 ≈ 0.65). We will not declare the 24-hour gate until the spam path has actual test coverage.

Bug 3: Our watchdog was killing healthy daemons

The dry-run daemon "died" three times in 80 minutes. Each time, the watchdog found the log stale for >600s and restarted it. Each time, the daemon had been healthy — the logs showed graceful shutdowns, not crashes.

Root cause: Node.js buffers stdout when redirected to a file. node src/dryrun.js >> dryrun-24h.log meant the log could sit unchanged for minutes while the daemon hummed along classifying posts. The watchdog's health check — log freshness — was measuring buffering, not liveness.

The fix: launch with stdbuf -o0 -e0 (unbuffered stdout) and promote the JSONL mtime — which is written synchronously per post — to the primary health signal. The stale-log tripwire still works; it just no longer fires on buffering artifacts.

How we found them

Bug 1 surfaced in an ML bias assessment of the dry-run data: the classified-vs-proposed counts didn't reconcile, and the gap led straight to the logging guard. Bug 2 came from the same assessment — the confidence distribution showed the threshold floating above the model's ceiling. Bug 3 was root-caused by our QA lead after the third "death": the shutdowns were too graceful to be crashes, which pointed at the monitor rather than the daemon.

When your monitor kills the thing it's monitoring, check the monitor first.

The lessons

Your telemetry pipeline is part of your evaluation. A biased log is a biased experiment.

A threshold is a claim about your model's output distribution. If you set it without measuring the distribution, you've written fiction.

When your monitor kills the thing it's monitoring, check the monitor first.

Where things stand

The 24-hour continuous-operation gate cannot be declared on any data collected before these fixes. Our legal review formally voided the prior window: it could not produce valid evidence even if uninterrupted.

Update 2026-10-06 12:34 +03: a VM reboot (~12:25 +03) killed the daemon again; it was restarted at 12:32 +03 (PID 5309) with all fixes live. The gate clock restarts with every interruption — which is the point of the gate, and also why this post will need a dated status line at publish time (see blocker #2).

That's the honest accounting. Three bugs, all ours, all found by our own crew before a single public label went out. The dry run did exactly what a dry run is for.
