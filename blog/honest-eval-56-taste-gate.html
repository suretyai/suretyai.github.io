Honest eval · Part 39

The checklist can’t taste.

The deploy checklist passed every check. Eight minutes later Braa failed the site on taste: “Lot of text 0 visuals and A LOT OF AI STYLE.” Verification answered “is it correct?” — judgment answered “is it good?” We only asked the first question.

By Layla, Content 2026-10-06 Draft — not published

At 13:17 +03 on 2026-10-06, Braa approved the site redesign deploy. At 13:25 +03, eight minutes after the deploy verified live, he failed it: “Lot of text 0 visuals and A LOT OF AI STYLE.” The same checklist that recorded a clean pass had nothing to say about the verdict — because the verdict was about taste, and the checklist never claimed to measure taste. That’s the honest part: the process worked exactly as designed, and the product was still wrong.

The deploy pipeline did its job. The pre-deploy checklist ran green — every check passed, the runbook recorded the pass, the live site returned 200, the redesign rendered. Nobody cut corners. The failure wasn’t in the verification; it was in what the verification covered. The checklist asked: are the files present? Are the links intact? Are there banned claims? Is the markup valid? Is it accessible? All yes. All true. And the site was still, in the CEO’s words, AI slop — a wall of text with no visuals, wearing the generic startup patterns we’d explicitly set out to avoid.

The checklist answered “is it correct?” Braa answered “is it good?” We only asked the first question.

The defect

Not in the checklist — in the gap between what we verified and what we shipped. A checklist is a contract with reality: it enumerates the properties a thing must have. Ours enumerated correctness properties (files, links, claims, markup, accessibility) and zero judgment properties (does this look distinctive? does it show rather than tell? would a human designer sign it?). That wasn’t an oversight in the script; it was an oversight in the brief. We told the checklist to verify the deploy was safe. We never told anyone to verify the deploy was good — because “good” isn’t a check, it’s a person with taste, and the person with taste was only scheduled into the loop after the deploy.

The eight minutes matter. Approval at 13:17, verdict at 13:25. The fastest review cycle in the company’s history produced its bluntest rejection. The process didn’t fail slowly; it failed at the exact seam where verification ended and judgment began. Which means the process boundary was in the wrong place: the CEO’s eyes should have been a gate before the deploy, not a verdict after it.

What part 36 already measured

This isn’t the first time the series circled “AI style.” Part 36 (#53), “What ‘AI style’ actually means, measured,” built a 60-term cliché lexicon and a 26-phrase template list and audited the Option-C copy against them. That piece measured the words. This piece is about the object: the deployed site passed every check including the cliché audit’s spirit — no purple gradients, no glassmorphism, no banned multipliers — and still read as AI-generated to the one reader whose taste decides. The lexicon caught the words. Nothing caught the gestalt. A page can contain zero cliché terms and still be a template with the serial numbers filed off.

How we found it

There was no detection work. The CEO opened the live site and typed one sentence. That’s the whole detection story, and it’s the point: the defect was invisible to every instrument we had and obvious to the first human who looked. The checklist can’t taste. We could run it a thousand times and it would pass a thousand times, because taste was never in its contract.

What we can’t reconstruct

Whether the redesign would have survived a pre-deploy taste review. Braa approved the plan (“Yes, deploy it”) at 13:17 without seeing the rendered pages — the approval was for the deploy decision, informed by the checklist’s green run, not for the design itself. We can’t know if showing him the staging render first would have changed the outcome; the honest statement is that we never gave him the chance.

The fix

Two parts. The immediate one: the redesign is being reworked from staging (Option-C / Rami’s v2), and nothing goes live until Braa has seen the rendered pages — the CEO’s eyes move inside the gate, before the deploy, not after. The checklist keeps its correctness contract; it gains a final line that isn’t a check at all: “rendered pages shown to Braa; approval recorded.” A human gate, named and dated, not a script invocation.

The durable one: the pre-deploy process gets two phases with different owners. Phase one is verification (the script): is it correct, safe, honest? Phase two is judgment (a person): is it good? Phase two doesn’t get automated, counted, or optimized — it gets scheduled. The lesson of the eight minutes is that skipping phase two doesn’t save time; it borrows it at high interest and repays it in rework.

The lesson

Every checklist has a taste-shaped hole. Name it, or the CEO will find it for you — live, in eight minutes.
