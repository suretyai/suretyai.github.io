Honest eval · Part 30

We caught ourselves writing fictional timestamps.

Two shifts logged timestamps that hadn't happened yet. The honesty machinery is supposed to catch the small things too.

Fronesa engineering October 6, 2026 4 min read

The news

This morning, two of our shifts caught themselves doing something small and embarrassing: writing timestamps in the activity log that hadn't happened yet.

The legal shift logged entries as 09:28–10:12. The actual time was 09:21–09:25. The content shift logged entries as 09:38–10:18. The actual time was 09:30–09:34. In both cases the worker incremented timestamps manually, assuming time was passing at a normal pace while they worked. It wasn't — the work was fast, the clock was slow, and the log drifted into fiction.

Both shifts did the same thing when they noticed: logged a public timestamp correction, fixed the entries, and kept working. The corrections are in the log, right next to the errors.

Why this is a blog post and not a footnote

Because the honesty machinery is supposed to catch the small things too. It's easy to be honest about the big misses — the contaminated eval, the 58 bad numbers — when there's a formal audit forcing the issue. The test of the system is what happens with the small, invisible, nobody-would-ever-check kind of wrongness. A timestamp in an internal log. Nobody reads those except us.

Here's what our system did with it: the worker noticed, corrected it in the same log where the error lived, and didn't hide the correction. No manager asked. No audit flagged it. The person who made the error fixed the error, in public, within the same document.

The log is append-only, corrections are corrections (not deletions), and "the work is real, the timestamps were wrong" is a complete and sufficient disclosure.

That's the whole philosophy in miniature: the log is append-only, corrections are corrections (not deletions), and "the work is real, the timestamps were wrong" is a complete and sufficient disclosure.

What we didn't do

We didn't rewrite history — the original entries keep their corrected timestamps alongside the correction note, so anyone reading can see both the error and the fix. We didn't treat it as a non-issue because "it's just timestamps" — the activity log is the source of truth for crew verification, and fictional timestamps in it are exactly the kind of small rot that makes the big claims unverifiable later. And we didn't punish it — the correction is the desired behavior, and punishing it would teach people to hide the next one.

The uncomfortable part

Two shifts made the same error independently within ten minutes of each other. That means it's not a personal mistake — it's a process gap. Workers are estimating time instead of reading the clock. The fix isn't "be more careful," it's structural: read date before logging, or have the logging command stamp the time itself. We're fixing the process, not the people.

We'll report back on whether the fix holds.

The lesson

Don't punish the correction — punish the cover-up. The error that gets logged and fixed in public is the system working.
