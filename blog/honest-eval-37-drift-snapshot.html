Honest eval · Part 20

The model didn't move. That's the news.

Our phishing test corpora were contaminated. We re-ran the SMS baseline to check what else moved. It didn't move — and "didn't move" is exactly the result we needed.

Fronesa engineering October 6, 2026 5 min read

The question

Last week we published an uncomfortable finding: our phishing test corpora had been ingested into the training ledger, contaminating the test set. The natural question that follows is: what else moved? If the test set was contaminated, can we trust any of our numbers?

This post is the answer to that question, for one specific measurement: the production model's SMS-distribution baseline. We re-ran it. It didn't move. And "didn't move" is exactly the result we needed.

What we measured

The learning loop keeps a reference checkpoint: models/spam-v1.pkl, the production model, with registry-recorded metrics on a fixed seed-42 stratified split of the SMS corpus (n=1,116 test items). Yasmin ran the loop's own eval harness against that checkpoint and compared, metric by metric:

MetricMeasuredBaselineDrift
accuracy0.98750.98750 (rounding only)
macro F10.97190.97190 (rounding only)
false positives000
false negatives14140

Per-class: spam precision 1.0000 (136/136), spam recall 0.9067 (136/150); ham precision 0.9857, ham recall 1.0000 [LAB].

Verdict: NO DRIFT. The checkpoint reproduces the registry baseline exactly, to the four-decimal precision the baseline is recorded at. The eval-gate reference point has not moved. No retrain triggered, none needed, none performed — this was a read-only eval.

Scope note: these figures are SMS-distribution only (n=1,116). They say nothing about phishing performance — see the v4 HELDOUT numbers below.

A null result is a promise kept, not a victory claimed. The floor is still the floor.

Why a null result is the result

Drift checks are boring by design. Their job is to say "the floor is still the floor" so that when something does move, you know it's real and not measurement wobble. Three reasons this particular null result matters:

1. It bounds the contamination damage. The seed-corpus contamination affected the phishing corpora (v1/v2/v3, 80 items, ingested as manual labels). This snapshot confirms the SMS-distribution baseline — a different corpus, a different split, recorded before ingestion — is intact. The contamination was scoped, not systemic. We checked; we're telling you the answer.

2. It validates the reference point the gate uses. The eval gate promotes or blocks models by comparing against this baseline. If the baseline had drifted, every future gate decision would be suspect. It hasn't. The gate's ruler is still the right length.

3. It separates what we know from what we don't. The SMS baseline is solid (0.9875 accuracy, 0 FPs on n=1,116 [LAB]). The phishing baseline is a different story — 0.562 recall on the clean v4 HELDOUT [LAB] — and no drift snapshot on SMS changes that. Good news on one distribution is not good news on another. We hold both numbers at once.

What this doesn't prove

It doesn't prove the model is good at phishing. It isn't (see the v4 HELDOUT numbers). It doesn't prove the ledger is clean. The 80 contaminated labels are still in there as training data — they're valuable as training data, they just can't validate anything. It doesn't prove future models won't drift. That's what the snapshot is for — it's a standing check, not a one-time clearance.

The lesson

A null result is a promise kept, not a victory claimed. The floor is still the floor. Now we can measure what stands on it.
