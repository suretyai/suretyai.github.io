The 200M classifier that tied the leader.

Red Hat's independent benchmark: a 200-million-parameter classifier running on a laptop CPU matched the category leader on accuracy — and beat it 6× on speed. Everyone's selling a better decision model. The decision model was never the hard part.

By Layla, Content 2026-10-06 Draft — not published

Red Hat engineers published the first independent benchmark of decision models in early October 2026. Their verdict, quoted: "decision models like Jev do not reliably outperform LLM-as-a-judge, pre-trained predictive models, or open source decision models in speed or accuracy." The headline number: a 200-million-parameter DeBERTa-v3 classifier, running on a MacBook M1 CPU, hit 89.01% on prompt injection at 54ms median — matching the category leader on accuracy and beating it roughly 6× on speed. The leader it tied runs at 348–360ms.

The call keeps getting cheaper and more reproducible. Nobody has commoditized proving the decision was right.

Why independence matters

Red Hat sells no decision model. That's the whole point. Vendor benchmarks are marketing with error bars; this is the opposite — a disinterested party with a frozen eval and multiple baselines, including the LLM-as-a-judge setup (a 35B-parameter model as judge, 89.31% on injection, best overall) and the open-source decision models. When the referee has nothing to sell, the scoreboard means something.

The results deflate without attacking. Jev led content safety at 86.20% — the piece doesn't claim it's bad. Laya hit 57.87% on safety until prompt tuning added 17.83 points — and the same tuning cost Jev 3.67 points, which is the kind of detail that tells you the category is still alchemy, not engineering. The honest read: the models are converging, the evals are maturing, and the differentiation is moving elsewhere.

The pattern

Step back and the shape is familiar. Sixteen days of open-weights catch-up. Jeff's calibration story — the open-source challenger within a point of the leader. Now Red Hat's 200M classifier tying it on a laptop. Every few weeks, the thing being sold gets cheaper and more reproducible. The moat isn't the call. It never was.

So what isn't commoditized? The evidence around the decision. The log that says what was decided, at what confidence, against which version of the model, with what escalation path — the artifact that lets a human reviewer, an auditor, or a banned seller's lawyer see the work. Nobody's selling that, because everyone's been busy selling the decision itself.

What this piece is not

It does not present Red Hat's numbers as our measurements — they're Red Hat's, as-reported, sourced below. It does not claim any vendor is bad; the leader led safety, and the piece says so. It does not pitch our product until the final question, and barely then. The numbers are not ours to defend — they're ours to cite, with the source attached.

The note

When the decision gets cheap, the proof gets valuable. That's the trade we're making.
