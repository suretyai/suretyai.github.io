Benchmarks

Measured in the lab. Published with the losses.

Every number on this page comes from a lab run we did ourselves, on public data, with the methodology written down next to it. Where we're weak, we say so. Nothing here is a customer result, a production guarantee, or a certification.

1 — Methodology

How we measure.

  • Data: Public datasets only — SMSSpamCollection for spam, TweetEval subsets for sentiment/hate/offensive. No customer traffic, no private data.
  • Splits: Stratified train/test splits, fixed seed (42), test sets held out from training.
  • Uncertainty: Error and F1 figures carry 95% bootstrap confidence intervals unless noted otherwise.
  • Calibration: ECE (Expected Calibration Error) reported alongside accuracy — a confident model that is wrong is worse than an uncertain one.
  • What “error” means: Wrong automatic decisions on the test set. Escalated items (routed to human review) are reported separately, not hidden inside the error rate.
  • Date of measurement: The headline numbers below were measured 2026-10-05 (rebuilt from the production model artifact with 2,000 bootstrap resamples). The DistilBERT head-to-head was measured 2026-10-05 on the same seed-42 split.
All figures on this page are lab measurements. They describe how the models behaved on these datasets, not how they will behave on your data.

DistilBERT wins on every metric. But the confusion matrices tell the fuller story:

Production: 966/966 ham correct (zero false positives)  ·  136/150 spam caught (14 missed)
DistilBERT: 960/966 ham correct (6 false positives)  ·  145/150 spam caught (5 missed)

DistilBERT catches 9 more spam messages but newly misflags 6 legitimate ones. Whether that trade is acceptable depends on the cost of a false positive in your use case — for some, a wrongly flagged legitimate message is worse than a missed spam. We publish both matrices so you can make that call yourself.

Deployment status: DistilBERT is evaluated, not deployed. The int8-quantized model is a 67.4 MB file, uses ~269 MB of RAM at inference time, and answers in ~5.5 ms (p50) instead of the production model's ~2 ms. The production API still serves the TF-IDF model.

Latency

The production model answers in about 1–2 ms on commodity CPU (p95 ~1.5 ms, measured on the same machine as the accuracy runs). Every API response includes a latency_ms field so you can verify this yourself on every call. These are warm-container numbers — the free tier sleeps after ~15 idle minutes, and a cold start can take up to ~90 s before the model even runs, which latency_ms does not measure. Exclude the first call after idle when comparing.

3 — Honest limitations

Where we're weak.

  • TweetEval hate speech: Our task-trained classifier performs near chance on the TweetEval hate subset. We do not recommend it for hate-speech detection.
  • TweetEval sentiment: Below the bar we would ship. Sentiment on short social text is a weak point.
  • TweetEval irony: Not measured at all. We will not claim coverage we haven't tested.
  • What works: SMS spam, IMDB reviews, AG News topic classification — short-to-medium English text with clear class boundaries.
  • Specialists, not generalists: These models are trained per task. They do not transfer to tasks they weren't trained on. That is a design choice, not a limitation we're hiding: a spam classifier should not be asked to judge sentiment.
  • English only: All measurements above are on English text. We have no multilingual numbers to show.
  • Lab ≠ your data: Class balance, slang, and adversarial text all move these numbers. Run your own evaluation — the API's escalated flag and confidence scores are there to help you find the boundary.
4 — FAQ

Asked, answered.

Are these numbers from real customer traffic?

No. All measurements are on public datasets in our lab. We have no customer results to report.

Is Fronesa certified for any regulated use?

No. No SOC 2, no ISO 27001, no regulatory certification of any kind. For high-stakes decisions, our API is designed to escalate to human review rather than decide alone — but the human-review step is your responsibility to staff.

Why do you escalate instead of just answering?

Because a wrong automatic decision is usually more expensive than a review. The API returns a confidence score with every decision and sets escalated: true when confidence falls below the bar. We would rather tell you “I don't know” than guess.

Can I reproduce these numbers?

The datasets are public (SMSSpamCollection, TweetEval), the splits use seed 42, and the methodology is described above. The DistilBERT training used distilbert-base-uncased for 3 epochs.

Do you train on customer data?

Not without written consent. Ever. See the data-handling page.

Why no competitor comparisons?

We benchmark against our own prior models on identical splits. We don't publish comparisons against other vendors' APIs because we can't control their side of the measurement — their models, rate limits, and pricing change without notice, and a comparison we can't keep honest isn't worth publishing.

5 — How to read this page
  • Figures with ± are 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.
  • “Error” means wrong automatic decisions; “escalation” is the share routed to human review. They are different things and we report both.
  • New claims must meet the multi-seed standard; single-seed results are labeled as such.
  • If a number on this page ever stops reproducing, we want to know — and we'll correct the page.
